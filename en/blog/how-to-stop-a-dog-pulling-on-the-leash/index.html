How to Stop a Dog Pulling on the Leash: Loose Leash Training

Updated October 5, 2026 · 6 min read

A dog that drags you down the street isn't bad or "dominant." It simply walks faster than you and has learned that pulling works: every time it pulled, it got closer to something interesting. The good news is that any dog can learn to walk on a loose leash. It takes clear rules, the right gear, and lots of repetition.

Why dogs pull on the leash

A dog's natural pace is faster than a human walk, and outside there are endless smells, dogs, and people. When your dog pulls and you follow, it gets a reward: it moves forward. Every walk like that makes pulling stronger. Pent-up energy plays a role too. A dog that spent all day lying at home bursts outside like a coiled spring, and even a well-trained dog will pull.

Gear: what helps and what doesn't

For training, use a standard leash about 5 to 6 feet long and a well-fitting Y-shaped harness that doesn't press on the throat or restrict the shoulders. A front-clip harness can reduce pulling in strong dogs. Put the retractable leash away while training, because it teaches the dog that tension means more room. We don't recommend choke or prong collars: they cause pain, can injure the neck, and teach nothing except stress. Bring a pouch of small, soft treats your dog can eat quickly.

Method 1: be a tree

The moment the leash goes tight, stop and stand still. Say nothing and don't jerk. As soon as your dog slackens the leash, for example by turning around or stepping back, say "yes" and keep walking. Your dog learns that pulling stops the walk and a loose leash moves it forward. On the first few walks you'll only cover a few yards, and that's normal. Consistency is everything: a single walk where pulling works again sets training back.

Method 2: reward the spot by your leg and change direction

Whenever your dog walks beside you on a loose leash, give a treat right next to your leg. At first every few steps, later less often. Your dog figures out that the spot beside you pays off. When it surges ahead, turn around and walk the other way. When it catches up, praise and reward. You can combine both methods, and they work for puppies and adult dogs alike.

How to make the training stick

Start at home or in the yard where there are few distractions, then move to a quiet street and finally to the park. Keep sessions short, ideally ten to fifteen minutes, and let your dog burn off energy first with fetch or play. Dogs also need time to sniff: give a clear cue such as "go sniff" when exploring is allowed, then bring your dog back to walking by your side. Expect reliable loose leash walking to take weeks or months. Basic commands help a lot, and we cover them in how to teach a dog basic commands.

The most common mistakes

The biggest mistake is inconsistency. If you tolerate pulling one day and not the next, your dog learns that trying is worth it. The second is jerking the leash, which hurts and solves nothing. The third is taking an under-exercised dog to train in a busy place where it can't succeed. And watch the whole family: if one person sticks to the rules and another lets the dog pull, training goes nowhere.

Some breeds find it harder. The Siberian Husky was bred to pull sleds, an energetic Labrador wants to be first everywhere, a Beagle follows its nose, and the Dalmatian, once a tireless coach dog, needs plenty of exercise before it settles. With them, expect to need extra patience. How many walks a dog needs is covered in how often to walk a dog, and you'll find more breeds in our dog breed dictionary.
