Dog vaccinations, microchipping and deworming: a beginner's guide

Updated November 16, 2026 · 5 min read

A microchip, a vaccine record and regular deworming are the basics of caring for any dog. For a new owner, though, it can feel like a jumble of dates and abbreviations. Here's a plain overview: what's usually required, when to do what, and roughly what it costs.

Microchipping: your dog's ID

A microchip is a tiny capsule about the size of a grain of rice that your vet injects under the skin between the shoulder blades. It hurts about as much as a regular shot and lasts for life. It carries a unique number that shelters and vets can scan when a lost dog turns up. Microchipping is mandatory in the UK and much of Europe. In the US it isn't required by federal law, but it's strongly recommended, and most shelters chip every dog before adoption.

The chip alone isn't enough. The number has to be registered with your contact details, otherwise nobody can link it to you. After you bring your dog home, check who the chip is registered to, and update the details whenever you move or change your phone number.

Vaccines: what your dog needs

Vets split vaccines into core ones that every dog should get and non-core ones that depend on lifestyle. The core combination shot, often labeled DHPP, protects against distemper, adenovirus (canine hepatitis), parvovirus and parainfluenza. Distemper and parvo are often fatal for puppies.

Rabies vaccination is required by law in most US states and is needed for travel almost everywhere. The first shot is usually given at 12 to 16 weeks, followed by a booster a year later and then every one to three years, depending on the vaccine and local rules.

Non-core vaccines include leptospirosis, which spreads through puddles, ponds and wildlife urine and is dangerous to people too, kennel cough (Bordetella), which most boarding kennels and daycares require, and Lyme disease in tick-heavy areas. Your vet will recommend what makes sense where you live.

First-year schedule

2–8 weeksdeworming every 2 weeks (done by the breeder)
6–8 weeks1st DHPP shot, microchip

14–16 weeks3rd DHPP shot, rabies
up to 6 monthsmonthly deworming, start heartworm prevention
12–16 monthsboosters for all vaccines

Exact dates depend on the vaccine brand and when your puppy started. Shots are usually 3 to 4 weeks apart. Until the series is complete, skip dog parks and other places full of unknown dogs, but don't keep your puppy locked away: socialization matters just as much at this age. For adult dogs, leptospirosis and kennel cough are usually given yearly, the core vaccines every one to three years. Ask your vet to keep everything in one vaccine record.

Deworming and parasite prevention

Many puppies are born with roundworms passed on from their mother, which is why deworming starts at two weeks old. Adult dogs are commonly dewormed several times a year or checked with a stool sample. Dogs that eat poop, hunt rodents or live with small children may need it more often. Always dose by your dog's current weight.

In the US, heartworm prevention is just as important. Mosquitoes spread heartworm in all 50 states, and most vets recommend a monthly preventive all year round, which often covers intestinal worms too. Add flea and tick protection as a chew, spot-on or collar.

What it costs

  • Microchip: about $25–75 one time, often less at shelters.
  • DHPP shot: about $25–50 per dose plus the exam; a puppy needs three or four.
  • Rabies: about $15–35, often less at low-cost clinics.
  • Dewormer: about $10–30 per dose, depending on weight.
  • Heartworm prevention: roughly $70–200 a year.

Prices vary a lot by clinic and region. Overall, budget roughly $300–600 for preventive care in your puppy's first year. For the full picture, see puppy costs in the first year.

Don't forget

  • When you pick up a puppy or adopt from a shelter, ask for the vaccine record and check the chip registration.
  • Put the due dates in your phone calendar. If a vaccine lapses for too long, the series may have to restart.
  • Your dog should be healthy, and ideally dewormed, on vaccination day.
  • A dog may be tired for a few hours after a shot. Facial swelling, vomiting or trouble breathing mean calling the vet right away.
